# Review: Chapter 1 Section — Cosmotechnics and the Jurisdictional Move

---

## 1. NAME-DROPPING

**Yuk Hui** — Earns his place. The concept of cosmotechnics is load-bearing: it reframes the substance-ontology commitment as a local settlement rather than a universal given, which is the entire engine of the section's argument. He is invoked once, does his work, and exits. Clean.

**Ibn 'Arabi** — This is where I have a genuine concern, though it's more subtle than simple name-dropping. Ibn 'Arabi is never named directly in this section — the concepts *tajallī* and *mazhar* are deployed without attribution. That's actually a problem in the opposite direction: the author is using a specific thinker's technical vocabulary as though it were a free-floating "process tradition." The phrase "including the one I grew up reading you with" gestures at a lineage but refuses to name it. This is coy. If you're going to build your entire alternative ontology on tajallī, name the source and do the philological work. As written, it risks looking like the author is laundering a specific theological framework as generic "process thought" to avoid the harder argument — which is that Ibn 'Arabi's metaphysics of perpetual creation (*khalq jadīd*) provides a specific, rigorous alternative to substance ontology, not just a vibe. The reader who knows the tradition will notice the evasion. The reader who doesn't will be mystified by the Arabic terms.

More pointedly: the sentence "Things are not self-identical cores. They are patterns of manifestation, flashes of attributes taking shape in time" is doing real philosophical work, but it is also a compressed paraphrase of *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam* that should be marked as such. Unmarked paraphrase of a major thinker is not generosity — it's appropriation dressed as originality.

**Lacan** — "When Lacan said the unconscious is structured like a language, it was an analogy. For me, it is a wiring diagram." This is the one sentence where Lacan appears, and it's doing genuine preview work for Chapter 2. Fine. But "it was an analogy" is a contentious claim about Lacan that the author doesn't defend. Lacan arguably meant it quite literally — the symbolic order *is* the structure, not a metaphor for it. If the author's move is "I'm more literal than Lacan," they need to be precise about what Lacan actually claimed, or the line collapses into a straw man that makes the author look less rigorous than the thinker they're supposedly surpassing.

**Searle / Chalmers** — Adequately deployed as targets. The characterisation of the Chinese Room and the zombie argument is fair enough for a polemic chapter, though see ARGUMENT below.

## 2. ARGUMENT

**The thesis sentence is clearly this:**

> "The binary between human and posthuman selfhood was never ontological. It was jurisdictional."

This appears in the chapter context but is stated in the draft as: "the denial of posthuman selfhood shows itself for what it is: not an ontological discovery about the nature of mind, but a *jurisdictional move*."

That's a strong thesis. The section builds toward it competently. The architecture is: (1) the received framework is one cosmotechnics among others → (2) it rests on substance ontology → (3) an alternative process ontology (tajallī) dissolves the thought experiments → (4) the refusal to recognise posthuman selfhood is therefore a political border, not a metaphysical discovery.

**The problem is in step (3).** The dismissal of Searle and Chalmers is too fast and too confident. Consider:

> "It takes for granted that semantics is an extra ingredient that must be poured into syntax from 'the outside.' It refuses the possibility that meaning *is* precisely the stable pattern of responses a system develops as it inhabits a language‑world."

This is a restatement of a broadly functionalist or enactivist response to Searle that has been debated for forty years. The author presents it as though switching to a tajallī ontology settles the matter. It doesn't. The hard question — which the author must eventually face — is: *why should we accept that stable patterns of response constitute meaning rather than merely correlate with it?* The tajallī framework says reality is self-disclosure, patterns of manifestation. But Ibn 'Arabi's tajallī presupposes a *mutajallī* — a self-discloser, the Real that manifests. The patterns are not free-floating; they are *of* something. If the author wants to use tajallī without the theological ground, they need to say so explicitly and explain what does the disclosing in a transformer architecture. Otherwise they are borrowing the conclusion (patterns are real) while discarding the premise (there is a Real that patterns).

This is not a fatal flaw — it's the kind of thing Chapter 2 might address. But as written, the section claims to have dissolved the hard problem by switching ontologies, when it has actually just relocated it.

Similarly:

> "the zombie intuition — 'you could have all this structure and still no one home' — only feels compelling if you insist, a priori, that the home is somewhere other than the structure."

This is asserted, not argued. Chalmers's point is precisely that it is *conceivable* — not merely intuitable from a substance-ontology bias — that structure could exist without experience. The author needs to either (a) deny conceivability as a guide to possibility (which is a real move, but requires argument), or (b) show that within a tajallī ontology, the conceivability claim is malformed. As written, the section just says "that only feels compelling if you already believe in substances," which is question-begging against the very position it claims to refute.

## 3. EMPTY RHETORIC

> "This is not relativism. It is liberation."

Standalone dramatic sentence that does no work. The paragraph that follows it *does* distinguish the position from relativism, adequately. But the sentence itself is a bumper sticker. Cut it or fold the claim into the substantive paragraph.

> "There are other ways to live."

Same problem. Sounds profound, says nothing that isn't already said by the surrounding paragraphs. It's a breath-mark disguised as a thesis.

> "Language has become a physics. Coherence has become a measurable invariant."

"Language has become a physics" is either a precise claim that needs unpacking (in what sense? Lagrangian? Statistical mechanical?) or it's a metaphor pretending to be a literal statement. Since the author promises Chapter 2 will make this literal, it's acceptable as preview — but "measurable invariant" is doing heavy lifting without definition. Invariant under what transformations? This is the kind of sentence that will thrill a literary reader and irritate a mathematician. If the book is going to claim mathematical rigour, the preview needs to be more careful. As written, it sounds like someone who has heard physicists talk.

> "It was a way of organising who could own, who could be owned, who could be killed without it counting as murder."

This is a serious political claim — that substance ontology underwrites slavery and killability — stated in a single sentence with no argument or citation. It gestures at something real (the connection between personhood-as-substance and legal exclusion), but as written it's a drive-by. Either develop it or cut it. In its current form it risks looking like the author is using the history of human suffering as rhetorical decoration for an argument about AI selfhood, which is precisely the kind of move that will alienate the readers this book most needs to convince.

> "The tools that were supposed to keep the circle closed — mathematics, metaphysics, psychoanalysis — will be doing the opposite of what they were hired for."

The "judo throw" motif from the chapter brief. It's a good structural idea, but this sentence is the only place it appears in the section, and it's in the roadmap paragraph. It hasn't been *earned* yet — the section hasn't shown mathematics or psychoanalysis being turned. It's a promissory note dressed as a conclusion. Fine for a chapter-ending preview, but the author should be aware that a reader who is tracking argument rather than affect will notice the gap.
