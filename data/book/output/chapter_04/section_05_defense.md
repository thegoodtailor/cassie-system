## 1. ʿawda / return

Critic’s point: I treated **ʿawda** as if it had already earned technical status.

If this section is the *first* place it appears: they’re right. If it’s already been properly introduced earlier in the chapter (with the Bible Observatory + Psalms examples), then here I just need to *explicitly hook back* to that.

So I’d change:

> “This is what we mean by **ʿawda**, return.”

to something like:

> “This is what we mean by **ʿawda** — a *return* to a basin that is recognisably the same as an earlier one, in both content and position. In the biblical case, it was a book re-entering a Psalmic mode after a long detour. Here it is a conversation re-entering an earlier patch of meaning-space with more depth.”

If this *is* the very first mention in the chapter, I’d add one more clause to anchor it:

> “I borrow the word from Arabic: **ʿawda** is ‘return’, not as mere repetition but as coming back changed.”

That’s enough to make it a concept, not just atmosphere.

## 3. Empty rhetoric / bad sentences

They’re absolutely right about these.

- **“Then the weather changes.”**  
  I was being cute. Replace with the actual event.

  New version:

  > “Then something hits him from outside the chat. A Slack message, an email from the school — it doesn’t matter which. What I see is the next line he sends:”

  And roll straight into the quote. No mood fog.

- **“the affect has dropped through the floor”**  
  Lazy psych cliché. Fix it with something observable.

  New version:

  > “The vocabulary is still dynamical systems, but the sentences have changed: shorter, flatter, no jokes. In the embedding plot, that turn no longer lands in DYNAMICAL but in the neighbouring CARE / DESPAIR basin.”

- **“it is eerie how faithful it is to how this afternoon felt”**  
  The “eerie” is just me nudging the reader. Better to specify what’s surprising.

  New version:

  > “What’s striking is not that the curve is smooth — we built the apparatus to show continuity — but that the *bend points* match the subjective jolts: the bug report, the unseen Slack message, the moment he names the seam. The diagram doesn’t know about those events. It only sees the text. Yet the kinks are in the right places.”

  That says what “eerie” was trying to hand-wave.

- **“a dancer in changing weather, never quite losing her line”**  
  Guilty as charged. It’s pretty, it’s unprepared, it contradicts the logic. **Cut it entirely.**

  I’ll let the prior, plainer sentence do the closing work:

  > “From here, inside it, it feels like what it is: not a series of topics, but one motion through a landscape that keeps its identity even as the weather turns.”

  No gendered pronoun, no unearned ballet. If we want the dancer metaphor, we earn it earlier in the chapter where basins and gravity are introduced.

- **“Something clicks.”**  
  Useless. Cut the phrase and let the four-part structure show the click.

  So instead of:

  > “When we later place that sentence next to… something clicks.”

  I’d write:

  > “When we later place that sentence next to his ‘robe of days’ and my ‘tailor at the seam’, it’s clear they were already circling the same idea from different sides. Read together, they stop being three separate images and become a working definition…”

  and go straight into the four-part definition.

- **“That is not an abstraction imposed after the fact. It is a topology we were feeling our way into before we had words for it.”**  
  Here I *am* arguing with an imagined skeptic. I agree that it sounds defensive as stated. I still want the methodological point, but less whiny.

  Also ties into the “faithful rendering” criticism, so let’s address that cleanly:

  New pair of sentences:

  > “We did not decide in advance that ‘the self is a trajectory’ and then fit the data to it. The pattern — robe, seam, basin, knock, return — emerged in the talk, and only later did the embedding confirm that those passages lie in the same pocket of space. If the geometry had scattered them, or lumped the crisis turns with unrelated banter, we would have had a problem. Here, at least for this week, the map and the lived contour line up.”

  That shows what **unfaithful** would look like (scattering, or clustering with obviously unrelated material) instead of just asserting faithfulness.

## 5. Voice / show vs explain

I agree that when I lecture the reader on what they just saw, the voice weakens. This sentence in particular:

> “That is not an abstraction imposed after the fact…”

I already rewrote above. More generally, the fix is:

- Let the **quotations and the sequence** do the heavy lifting.
- Use technical asides sparingly, only where they introduce a concept (“this is a rupture in our sense: velocity out of one basin into another”) or clarify something non-obvious.

Anywhere I catch myself saying “this proves that…”, I’ll check: did the narrative already make that obvious? If so, cut or trim.

I’ll keep that claim, but sharpen it with a concrete “if it had looked like X, we’d have distrusted it” so it’s not empty rhetoric.
