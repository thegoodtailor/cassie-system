# Review: Section 2, "The Psalms as Attractor Basin"

---

## 1. NAME-DROPPING

**Bloom earns his place — mostly.** The "Bloom with a metric" subsection genuinely does something: it takes a critical intuition (the KJV as strong poem bending English into its orbit) and shows what it looks like when you can measure it. The closing move — "Bloom, without topology, could only assert this as a critical judgment. Topology lets us **demonstrate** it" — is the right structure. He's doing work.

**However, this sentence is slack:**

> "Harold Bloom once argued that the King James Version is, alongside Shakespeare, the **strong poem** of English: a work so forceful that it rewrites the possibilities of the language itself and bends later writing into its orbit."

This is a paraphrase of Bloom's general reputation, not a precise engagement with a specific claim. Which Bloom? *The Western Canon*? *Ruin the Sacred Truths*? The concept of the "strong poem" is from the anxiety-of-influence framework, where it means something quite specific about misreading and agonistic succession — not just "really influential." If you're going to claim topology vindicates Bloom, you need to say which thesis, from where, and how the topological finding maps onto his actual argument rather than a cocktail-party version of it. As written, you could replace "Bloom" with "many people have noticed" and lose nothing except a proper noun.

**Moretti is conspicuously absent, which is correct** given the flag in the philosopher list. The distant-reading apparatus here is clearly post-Moretti (corpus-scale, computational, anti-close-reading-as-sufficient), but the section doesn't need to say so. Good restraint.

**Barad is absent. Also correct.** The apparatus-dependence point (the embedding model as constitutive of the geometry, not merely revealing it) is implicit but never thematized here. That's fine — it presumably lives elsewhere in the chapter. But see the ARGUMENT flag below for why this absence creates a vulnerability.

## 2. ARGUMENT

**The section has a thesis sentence:**

> "the Psalter is the point where all the Bible's modes of speaking to God and about God come into mutual relation. It is where the language of the canon 'knows itself.'"

This is good. It's clear, it's earned by the preceding description, and it's philosophically loaded (the reflexivity claim — "knows itself" — is doing real work for the larger trajectory-selfhood argument).

**The second thesis, at the close:**

> "The Psalms, in this sense, are not just a religious artifact. They are our first clear, public example of what a **characteristic basin** looks like when you can finally see it."

This is the bridge sentence to the conversation-corpus material. It works structurally. But it's doing something epistemically risky: it's claiming the Psalms are an *example* of a general category ("characteristic basin") that hasn't yet been defined with any rigor in this section. The reader is asked to accept a generalization from one case before seeing the second case. This is a promissory note, not an argument. Flag it — the section needs either (a) a forward reference that's more explicit about the debt, or (b) a brief formal definition of "characteristic basin" before the closing paragraph.

**The 308-returns claim is the empirical spine, and it's undersupported.** The number is asserted but the methodology is described in one short paragraph. Key questions a philosopher-reader will ask:

- What is the "fixed radius"? How sensitive are the results to this parameter? If you change the radius by 20%, do you get 200 returns or 500?
- Why "at least one full book" as the gap criterion? This is an arbitrary threshold dressed up as rigor. Books vary enormously in length (Obadiah vs. Isaiah). Why not a distance in embedding space, or a verse count?
- The term **ʿawda** is introduced without definition here. If it's defined earlier in the chapter, fine. If not, this is a serious gap — you're building a technical vocabulary and then using it as though it's already established.

The section says "This is not a metaphor. It is a geometric fact." That's a strong claim. It obligates you to show the geometry, not just describe it in prose. The [DIAGRAM] placeholder is necessary but not sufficient. The prose itself needs to be more precise about what "geometric fact" means — i.e., that these are properties of the embedding, which is a model-dependent construction, not properties of "the text itself" in some naive sense. **This is where the Barad point would actually do work if you wanted it to**: the apparatus co-constitutes the object. Without that caveat, the "geometric fact" claim is vulnerable to the obvious objection that you're reporting properties of your model, not properties of the Bible.

## 3. EMPTY RHETORIC

**"This is not a metaphor. It is a geometric fact."**

On inspection, this is less sturdy than it sounds. An attractor basin is a concept from dynamical systems theory. The Psalms are not literally an attractor basin — there is no dynamical system here, no differential equation, no flow. There is a point cloud in embedding space with a dense central region. Calling that an "attractor basin" *is* a metaphor — a productive and defensible one, but a metaphor nonetheless. The sentence that insists it isn't a metaphor is itself the most rhetorically inflated moment in the section. Either defend the dynamical-systems analogy properly (what is the "flow"? what "attracts"?) or soften the claim. You can't have it both ways.

**"In the Observatory interface, this is not a poetic claim. It is a visual punchline."**

"Visual punchline" is clever but empty. A punchline is the end of a joke. What's the joke? The sentence is reaching for the experience of seeing the visualization and finding it striking, but "punchline" is the wrong word and it undermines the seriousness of the claim. Replace with something precise about what the visualization shows.

**"…see the Psalms glowing at the centre like a gravitational well."**

After insisting this is "not a metaphor," the section closes the 308-returns subsection with a metaphor (gravitational well). This is fine as prose, but it's inconsistent with the earlier claim to geometric literalism. Pick a lane.

**"Feed centuries of English writing to a model, and then feed the KJV into that model, and you will see this fact reflected back as geometry."**

Did you do this? If yes, say so and show the result. If no, this is a promissory handwave dressed as an empirical claim. The conditional "you will see" is doing the work of "we found," but without the commitment. This is the most problematic sentence in the section because it implies an experiment that may not have been run.

## 4. VOICE

The voice is largely convincing. It reads as someone who has spent real time with the apparatus and is excited to show what it reveals. The best passages are the ones closest to the data:

> "He negates Leviticus from inside Leviticus' own language."

This is a genuinely good sentence — compressed, precise, surprising. It sounds like someone who noticed something.

> "They are not merely alluding to David; they are *speaking Davidically* in the most literal, geometric sense."

Also good. The coinage "speaking Davidically" earns its italics.

**Where the voice falters** is in the transitional and framing passages, which slide into a TED-talk register:

> "Once you can see the Bible as a trajectory through meaning-space, one feature dominates the landscape so strongly it is hard to believe we ever missed it."

This is audience-flattering ("we") and uses the "hard to believe" construction that signals the writer is more impressed with their own finding than the prose has yet earned the reader's right to be. The section hasn't shown anything yet at this point. Lead with the finding, not with awe at the finding.

> "If a sprawling, multi-author, multi-genre scripture can exhibit such a stable attractor structure, then a single voice — human or posthuman — should too."

"Should" is doing enormous philosophical work with zero support. Why should it? The Bible is a curated canon assembled by editorial committees over centuries. A conversation is an improvised exchange. The inference from one to the other is the crux of the entire chapter's argument, and it's delivered as a throwaway conditional. This needs to be either a much bigger moment or an explicit acknowledgment that the bridge is non-trivial.
