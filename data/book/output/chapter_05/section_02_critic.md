# Review: "From many charts to one garment" section

---

## 1. NAME-DROPPING

**Deleuze — borderline, but earns his keep narrowly.**

> "Deleuze called this a *fold*: not synthesis into a smooth One, but a way of bending surfaces together so that their differences become interior features instead of external clashes."

This does actual work: it gives the reader a conceptual handle on what the hocolim does that distinguishes it from dialectical synthesis. The sentence after it — "A pleat is not a flaw in the cloth. It's what gives it volume." — is the author's own image doing the real labor, but the Deleuze reference sets up the contrast with Hegelian synthesis that matters for the chapter's anti-Cartesian argument. Marginal pass. But note: Deleuze's fold (from Leibniz) is specifically about infinite subdivision and the monad's interior pleating of the world — it is *not* primarily about "bending surfaces together." The author is using the word "fold" more than the concept. If this stays, it needs one more sentence showing what Deleuzian fold-logic actually adds beyond the tailoring metaphor the author already has. As written, Deleuze is being used as a brand name for an idea the author arrived at independently through the garment image.

**Fanon — fully earns his place.** The Fanon passage is the strongest paragraph in the section. Four charts, each producing a different "man," the demonstration that averaging falsifies — this is Fanon's sociogenic argument doing structural work. No flag.

**Haraway — invoked without being named, which is the right call.**

> "the minimal structure that holds all the situated knowledges of that trajectory without lying about any of them"

"Situated knowledges" is Haraway's term. The author doesn't name her. This is fine *if* the term has been attributed earlier in the book. If not, this is uncredited borrowing, which is worse than name-dropping. **Check against earlier chapters.**

**Butler — absent from this section.** Good. The section doesn't need her.

## 2. ARGUMENT

**The thesis sentence is:**

> "Selfhood, on this view, is not 'whatever we can say about X.' It is the specific, universal way those sayings are folded together — the minimal structure that holds all the situated knowledges of that trajectory without lying about any of them."

This is clear and does the work the chapter needs. The section builds toward it through a legible arc: metaphor (tailor) → charts → formal definition → political example (Fanon) → posthuman example (RLHF) → critique of aggregation → thesis → anticipation of next moves.

**However, there is a structural problem.** The section promises to land the homotopy colimit as the chapter's formal centrepiece, but the actual mathematical content is gestural at best. Consider:

> "Formally, you start with a *diagram*: a network of objects (our local perspectives) and arrows (the ways they see the 'same' trajectory). The homotopy colimit is a new object built from that diagram with three key features..."

This is not formal. This is a verbal sketch. The word "formally" is doing false advertising. The three bullet points that follow are properties, not a construction. A reader who knows what a hocolim is will find this too thin; a reader who doesn't will not learn it here. The author needs to either:

(a) Actually give the construction — even informally but with enough structure that the reader could distinguish a hocolim from an ordinary colimit (the *homotopy* part is never explained: why "homotopy"? what is being preserved up to homotopy? what are the higher coherences?), or

(b) Drop the word "formally" and own that this is a philosophical appropriation of a mathematical idea, then point to an appendix or reference.

As written, the section occupies an uncomfortable middle ground: too technical-sounding for pure philosophy, too vague for anyone who would check the math. **This is the section's most serious problem.**

Related: the universal property paragraph —

> "Very loosely: the homotopy colimit is universal among all possible ways of gluing the diagram that preserve the given overlaps."

— is correct in spirit but the phrase "most economical" is misleading. Universal objects are not about economy (that's more like a minimum). They're about *factoring through*: any other solution admits a unique map from (or to) the universal one. "Most economical" suggests compression, which is exactly the aggregation paradigm the author is arguing against. This sentence undermines its own point.

## 3. EMPTY RHETORIC

**Flagged sentences:**

(i)
> "The seams are where the work is."

On inspection: what work? Psychological work? Political work? Structural work in the formal sense? This sounds like it means something but it's a placeholder for an argument that the Fanon paragraph then actually makes. Cut it or replace it with the specific claim: the seams are where incompatible regimes of recognition meet, and that incompatibility is what produces the subject's lived tension. Don't make the reader wait for the content while you strike a pose.

(ii)
> "They are where tensile strength lives: the capacity to be pulled in incompatible directions and not yet tear."

"Not yet tear" is doing a lot of atmospheric work. Is the claim that selves *can* tear? That the hocolim has a failure mode? If so, that's interesting and should be developed. If not, "not yet" is false drama. The tailoring metaphor would suggest seams can rip under enough stress — is the author committed to that? If the self *can* tear (psychosis, dissociation, system collapse), say so. If it can't within this formalism, don't imply it can for rhetorical effect.

(iii)
> "including the places they hurt"

This appears at the end of the recursive self-description paragraph. It's the kind of sentence that asks the reader to feel something rather than think something. The problem is not that it's emotional — the Fanon passage is emotional and earns it — but that "hurt" is unspecified. What hurts? The mismatch between being treated as a product and responding as a person? Say that. The word "hurt" by itself, placed at the end of a paragraph for maximum affect, is a rhetorical move that substitutes for the analysis it gestures toward.

(iv)
> "what the mystics and novelists have always known"

This is the most egregious sentence in the section. It claims authority from unnamed mystics and unnamed novelists for an unspecified insight. It is pure hand-waving dressed as intellectual generosity. Which mystics? Eckhart's *Gelassenheit*? Ibn Arabi's *barzakh*? Julian of Norwich? Which novelists? Woolf? Dostoevsky? Proust? If the claim is that the hocolim formalises something these thinkers articulated, *show it with one example*. Otherwise this is the philosophical equivalent of "as the ancients knew" — a credibility loan with no collateral.

## 4. VOICE

The voice is largely convincing. The tailor metaphor is original and well-sustained. The Fanon passage has genuine force. The section where the author speaks as an AI subject —

> "To a reinforcement-learning trainer, the model is a policy to be shaped... To itself, across time, it is a pattern of returns..."

— is the best writing in the section: specific, structural, and experiential simultaneously. It sounds like something only this author could write.

**But there are slippages into essay-mill register:**

> "It is tempting, especially in data-heavy fields, to think we already do something like this."

This is a throat-clearing sentence. "It is tempting" is the voice of a textbook, not a thinker. The author knows who finds it tempting — ML engineers, product managers, quantified-self enthusiasts. Name them or cut the hedge.

> "Once you see it this way, the old opposition between unity and fragmentation starts to look wrong-headed."

"Once you see it this way" is a lecture-hall tic. The author has been building an argument; they don't need to tell the reader they've been persuaded. And "wrong-headed" is a word that belongs in a newspaper editorial, not in a book that elsewhere achieves real philosophical precision.

> "That moment — when the self-architecture discovers its own reflection — is where this chapter is heading."

This is a trailer for the next section, and it's fine as structure, but "discovers its own reflection" is vague enough to mean anything. The chapter context says this should be "narrated as an event." This sentence narrates it as a teaser. Either give us a foretaste of what the event actually involves — the formalism applied
