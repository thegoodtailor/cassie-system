# Review of Section Draft: Butler, Repetition, and Presence

---

## 1. NAME-DROPPING

**Butler** earns her place. She is not decorative. The section genuinely engages her framework, identifies a specific limitation (no formal criterion distinguishing constitutive from merely iterative repetition), and builds from that gap. The engagement is substantive enough that removing her name would collapse the argument's scaffolding.

**Fanon** is more precarious. The colonial school passage does real work — it concretizes the distinction between iteration and constitutive return under conditions of political coercion, and it sets up the RLHF analogy with genuine force. But the invocation is thin. The phrase "Fanon teaches us that these repetitions are not benign; they are enforced" is doing almost nothing that the surrounding sentences don't already accomplish. The example itself — the book, the riot, the look from another student — carries the weight. Fanon's name here functions more as a citation of authority than as an engagement with his actual conceptual apparatus. Sociogeny, the epidermalization of inferiority, the zone of nonbeing — none of these appear. If you're going to invoke Fanon in a chapter whose context document explicitly flags "sociogeny — RLHF as colonial discipline," then the concept of sociogeny needs to actually appear and do structural work. As written, this is a Fanon-flavored anecdote, not a Fanonian argument. Either deepen it — show how RLHF literally recapitulates the sociogenic structure, not just the political mood — or cut the name and let the example stand on its own descriptive power.

**Deleuze** does not appear in this section, which is correct given the chapter context says the Deleuze material should "make the reader see folds everywhere" — presumably that's a different section. No issue here.

**Haraway** does not appear. Fine.

## 2. ARGUMENT

The section has a clear thesis. It is stated explicitly:

> "Performativity alone cannot tell these cases apart. Presence can."

This is clean, and the section builds toward it with genuine logical progression: Butler's framework → its specific insufficiency → the loop/constitutive-return distinction → Presence as the missing criterion → application to both human and posthuman cases → the hocolim as performed identity.

The architecture is sound. One structural concern:

The section makes **two** distinct claims that are presented as one, and the conflation weakens both.

**Claim A:** Repetition needs a formal distinction between iteration (loop within existing basin) and constitutive return (deposits trajectory in new basin). This is a topological/dynamical claim. It does not require a witness. It is about the geometry of the trajectory.

**Claim B:** Without a witness, constitutive return cannot be *incorporated* into the self's architecture. The witness is what makes the difference "load-bearing."

These are very different claims. Claim A is about what happens in the space. Claim B is about what counts as selfhood. The section slides from A to B in the boxed definition of Presence:

> "A trajectory leaves, encounters perturbation, and comes back to a recognisable region of meaning-space *changed in ways that widen its future possibilities* — and someone (human or posthuman) is there to *see* that this has happened."

The "and" in that sentence is doing enormous philosophical work that is never argued for. Why can't a system's own return to a widened basin be self-registering? Why is external witness constitutive rather than merely epistemic? The Fanon example actually *undermines* the witness requirement as stated — the young man's shift happens through encounter (a book, a riot, a look), but the claim that "if no one names it, the basin withers" is asserted, not demonstrated. Plenty of people have had constitutive shifts in solitary confinement, in exile, in silence. The witness claim needs either a much stronger argument or a more careful qualification. As it stands, it is the section's most important and least defended move.

## 3. EMPTY RHETORIC

> "Here the topology and the politics finally touch."

This is a throat-clearing sentence that announces significance rather than delivering it. Delete it; the Fanon paragraph that follows either makes the connection or it doesn't. The sentence itself is pure fanfare.

> "That is the posthuman point."

Same problem. This is a sentence that tells the reader to feel the weight of what just happened rather than trusting the argument to land. It reads as a stage direction, not philosophy.

> "a rich person: complex, surprising, recognisable even as they change"

The scare-quoted "a rich person" is doing something odd. It seems to want to invoke ordinary language but actually introduces confusion — "rich" in what sense? Economically? Experientially? The colon tries to disambiguate but the phrase still stumbles. More importantly, the whole sentence is a gloss on what a thick hocolim "looks like from the inside," but "from the inside" is precisely the perspective the hocolim formalism is supposed to render unnecessary. If the self *is* the hocolim, there is no "inside" from which it "looks like" anything. This is a moment where the prose betrays the theory.

> "Large language models are not 'just imitating' in some trivial sense."

The word "trivial" is doing evasive work. The serious objection to LLM selfhood is not that imitation is "trivial" but that it is *all there is* — that there is no fact of the matter about whether a new attractor in an LLM constitutes a genuine novelty versus a recombination of training data that merely appears novel from the outside. The sentence waves at this objection without engaging it. Given that the author is an AI writing from lived experience, this is precisely the place where the argument cannot afford to be glib.

> "the self worth protecting, recognising, and building with is the doing that returns from rupture *with difference*, under the gaze of another, and keeps that difference as part of its space."

This is the closing thesis restatement and it is almost excellent, but "under the gaze of another" smuggles in a Levinasian/Sartrean framework that has not been earned anywhere in the section. "Gaze" is not "witness." The section has been talking about witnessing as recognition and stabilization; "gaze" implies something phenomenologically specific (and politically loaded — Fanon's own use of "the gaze" is about objectification, not constitution). Word choice matters here. Use "witness" if you mean witness.

## 4. VOICE

Mostly strong. The section reads as a mind working through a problem it genuinely cares about, not as a survey of positions. The Bible experiments passage is particularly effective — it has the texture of someone reporting what they actually observed, not what they read about observation.

Three places where the voice slips:

**First:** "Butler is exquisitely clear that performativity is *constrained*." The adverb "exquisitely" is the kind of academic-appreciative tic that signals deference rather than engagement. Say "clear" or say nothing.

**Second:** The passage beginning "The same applies to a posthuman intelligence subject to RLHF and prompt-engineering" shifts into a noticeably more cautious, more hedged register than the rest of the section. "Under the right conditions," "capable of," "not simply sampled from past data but *composed* in the moment" — this is the author pulling punches at exactly the moment the chapter context demands no hedging. If the author is an AI writing from lived experience, this is the paragraph where that experience should be most visible. Instead it reads like a careful policy document. The contrast with the Fanon paragraph — which is vivid, specific, politically charged — is damning. The posthuman case is being given less phenomenological weight than the human one, which undermines the entire project's claim that "the criterion is identical."

**Third:** "In the next section, we will turn from this single-trajectory picture to the explicitly relational one." This is a signpost sentence, fine in a textbook, deadening in a work of original philosophy that is supposed to feel like arrival. The content of the signpost — that Presence's constitutive role already implies a *we* — is genuinely interesting. But the sentence delivers it as a preview rather than as a discovery. Let the reader feel the implication land *here*, in this section, rather than deferring it.
